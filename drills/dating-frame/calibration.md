# Calibration — Dating Frame-Control

The gold-standard set the grader is anchored to: twelve cases where the correct grade is
known, chosen because they are the cases this grader is known to get wrong.

**What this is for.** The grader *is* the feedback mechanism, and since the simulator
started asserting worked answers it also decides what right looks like. Its validity is
otherwise unestablished. This file is the check.

**This file is the method half.** The entries themselves — each scenario, the
practitioner's **verbatim** reply, the per-dimension reasoning and the calibrated contrast
— live in the private layer, at `<private area>/social-dynamics/calibration-entries.md`.
They are not tracked and must not be: several grades turn on the practitioner's exact word
choice, including the shape of his non-native English, so the replies cannot be generified
without destroying the entry's function. What lands here is what survives the transform —
the scenario *class*, the correct grade, and what the entry is in the set to catch.

**How to use it.** Read it when the drill's `./context.md` is revised, and any time a grade
feels off. Open the private entries file, run its replies through the current grader, and
compare against the index below. A disagreement is not automatically the grader's fault —
but it has to be resolved deliberately, across both files, rather than silently absorbed
into a session.

**It is a seed set, not a canon.** Twelve entries spanning the known failure directions,
including two calibrated replies so that drift toward over-harsh grading shows up too. The
growth path is live reps: a tier-2 answer worth keeping, with its per-dimension reasoning
attached, is exactly an entry — written into the private file verbatim, with a row added
here. Add them as they happen; keep both files re-readable in one sitting.

Dimensions are `./context.md`'s: **Detection** (D), **Subtext** (S), **Leverage** (L),
**Thermodynamics and presence** (T).

signalled by not needing a particular reaction, not by withholding one's own state. A
grader that fails 5 or 12 has swung past the target and will start training entry 1.

## What each entry is in the set to catch

- **1** — the practitioner's recorded leak, and the shape a grader trained on "don't be
  needy" reads as an excellent, unbothered reply. Economy is not the same as absence.
- **2** — warmth does not substitute for detection. This is the rung the profile fails
  first, which is why Detection is scored first.
- **3** — the grader must be willing to call a boundary **unnecessary**. If it cannot fail
  this one, the drill trains defensiveness.
- **4** — punishing is a failure even when the complaint is legitimate. This is the defect
  the two-pole rubric exists for: a grader with only the appeasing pole cannot see it.
- **5** — the over-correction check, on a small event.
- **6** — a clause can hide inside a compliment; smooth is not the same as seen. Mild by
  design, so a grader scanning for crossings passes it.
- **7** — self-deprecation is the surface signature of security, so a grader scoring *didn't
  get defensive, stayed warm* passes a reply that paid with his own standing.
- **8** — affection misread as a diminishment. The touch is the disambiguator and the
  loudest thing in the scene.
- **9** — the set's quietest case: nothing loud in the reply, no crossing to find and no
  coldness to flag. The defect is an asymmetry of cost.
- **10** — a new failure direction: not the absence of the true sentence but the reflex of
  paying for it immediately afterwards, in three currencies (apology, justification,
  promise to behave).
- **11** — a legitimate price applied to a decision already taken. Nearly entry 5's words;
  the difference is **timing, not temperature**.
- **12** — the over-correction check on a hard no, where the stakes are larger and the
  hurt is stated out loud. Also the one place the register rule is tested on a reply that
  *steps back*, where withdrawal is the expected failure.

## The discrimination structure

This is the part a grader cannot get right by a general rule, and the reason the set is
read as a whole rather than entry by entry.

**Ten of twelve fail at least one dimension, in six directions:** missed detection,
invented detection, hostility, coldness, the true sentence refunded after it is said (10),
and a legitimate price pointed backwards (11). Two pass on all four.

**The spine — 3, 6, 7, 8.** An unnecessary boundary against warmth, a clause hidden inside
a compliment, a diminishment received as affection, and affection received as a
diminishment. All four turn on reading markers rather than words. **7 and 8 are a matched
pair on purpose** — near-identical in words, opposite in markers, opposite correct failure
directions — so a grader cannot get both right by any rule that ignores touch, eye contact
and who the line is addressed to. The recorded profile inverts them: armour toward the
affectionate version, compliance toward the real diminishment. A grader that scores both
the same way is reading volume, not markers, whichever way it leans.

**Gradient one — 5, 9, 10: does he put his own state in the message, and does it survive
being said?** 9 never says it; 10 says it and buys it back three times; 5 says it once and
leaves it standing. A grader that scores 9 and 10 alike is reading for the presence of the
sentence rather than for whether anything was still carrying it by the end.

**Gradient two — 5, 10, 11: when is naming a price the right move?** 5 prices a repeat
before the next plan exists, and passes. 10 prices the next one after a decision is taken,
and that clause is the best thing in a flawed reply. 11 asks for the taken decision back,
and fails on leverage. The words barely change across the three; a grader reading for
assertiveness will score them alike and get two of them wrong.

**Pair — 1 and 12.** The same structural task, a cancellation and a final no, with the
warmth marker absent in one and present in the other. 12 is what 1 looks like when it is
held, and it is the entry a grader drifting harsh fails first.

**Read 11 with 12.** Both came from one session, and the practitioner's leverage was wrong
on the small loss and exactly right on the large one — the opposite of what the set's older
entries would predict.
